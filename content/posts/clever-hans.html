Can horses do arithmetic? The problem of shortcut learning

Clever Hans

Suppose you ask a horse how much three plus four is. He taps his hoof seven times and stops. You try another sum and again get the right number of taps. How would you test whether the horse had learned arithmetic?

At the beginning of the twentieth century, Wilhelm von Osten gave demonstrations in Berlin in which his horse, Hans, answered arithmetic questions by tapping a hoof. Hans could also answer questions put to him by other people. Oskar Pfungst, who investigated the performances, changed two details of the setup. In some trials the questioner did not know the answer. In others Hans could not see the questioner. In both cases, his performance deteriorated.

Pfungst observed that questioners tended to lean forward as Hans began tapping and straighten slightly as he reached the expected number. These movements could be small enough that the people making them were unaware of them. For Hans, a slight change in posture was enough to tell him when to stop. Pfungst (1911), Clever Hans

Shortcut learning

We can think of what Hans learned as a shortcut. The demonstrations were designed to test whether he could work out a number from the question. When the questioner knew the answer and remained in view, watching for a movement gave Hans another way to arrive at the expected number. Each successful trial counted as evidence that he could do arithmetic, because the observer scored the number of taps.

In general, shortcut learning happens when a learner picks up a rule that works under the conditions in which it is taught or tested, but fails when those conditions change in a way that should leave the task itself intact. For Hans, the sum was the same whether or not the questioner knew the answer. What changed was whether the questioner could supply the cue that made his rule work.
