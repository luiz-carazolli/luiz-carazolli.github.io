Clever Hans

Suppose you ask a horse how much three plus four is. He taps his hoof seven times and stops. You try another sum and again get the right number of taps. How would you test whether the horse had learned arithmetic?

At the beginning of the twentieth century, Wilhelm von Osten gave demonstrations in Berlin in which his horse, Hans, answered arithmetic questions by tapping a hoof. The horse's accuracy was so impressive he would draw crowds of thousands of people that wanted to see an animal preforming human-like intellectual tasks.

In 1907, skeptical about this spectacle, psychologist Oskar Pfungst started investigating the performances, changing two details of the setup: In some trials the questioner did not know the answer, whereas in others Hans could not see the questioner. In both cases, his performance deteriorated. Pfungst solved the mystery by observing that questioners tended to lean forward as Hans began tapping and straighten slightly as he reached the expected number. These movements could be small enough that the people making them were unaware of them, but for Hans, a slight change in posture was enough to tell him when to stop. Pfungst (1911), Clever Hans

Shortcut learning

We can think of what Hans learned as a shortcut. The demonstrations were designed to test whether he could work out a number from the question. When the questioner knew the answer and remained in view, watching for a movement gave Hans another way to arrive at the expected number. Each successful trial counted as evidence that he could do arithmetic, because the observer scored the number of taps.

In general, shortcut learning happens when a learner picks up a rule that works under the conditions in which it is taught or tested, but fails when those conditions change in a way that should leave the task itself intact. For Hans, the sum was the same whether or not the questioner knew the answer. What changed was whether the questioner could supply the cue that made his rule work.

Shortcuts in deep neural networks

In Shortcut Learning in Deep Neural Networks, Robert Geirhos and his collaborators describe the same pattern in machine learning. One of their examples is a neural network trained to distinguish pictures of stars from pictures of moons. The objects could appear in different parts of the image, but the dataset was constructed so that stars tended to occupy two corners and moons the other two. Since both shape and position gave information about the label, the network could achieve high accuracy on new images drawn from the same dataset by learning where each kind of object usually appeared.

To see which rule the network had learned, the authors tested it on images in which stars and moons could appear anywhere. A star kept its shape when it moved to a corner usually occupied by moons, but the network’s accuracy dropped to about chance once position stopped predicting the label. In both this example and the earlier demonstrations with Hans, getting the right answer was compatible with two different rules, and changing the conditions of the test made it possible to tell them apart. Spurious correlations

What made the shortcuts available in both cases was a spurious correlation. A feature is spuriously correlated with a label when it helps predict that label in the examples we observe because of how those examples were produced, even though the task can stay the same when that association disappears. In Hans' demonstrations, the questioner knew the answer and would tend to straighten at the moment when it was correct for Hans to stop tapping. As a result, the movement was informative about whether the current tap was the last one, and Hans could use it to get the right answer. In Geirhos' paper, the positioning of the images served as a cue for the model to distinguish the two objects.

An experiment with handwritten digits

Suppose we are training a model to recognize handwritten digits, and most of the 7s we show it are on blue backgrounds. When we give the model a new blue 7 and it predicts 7, we face the same difficulty as someone watching Hans tap his hoof. The handwriting and the background both point toward the same answer, so a correct prediction leaves us wondering how much each contributed. With an image, though, we can keep the handwriting exactly as it is and change the background to the color usually associated with a 2. If the model now predicts 2, we know which part of the image changed and why that particular wrong answer is interesting.

The full experimental protocol, code, and detailed analysis are available in the Colored MNIST shortcuts repository.

Giving each digit a preferred color

We will use MNIST, a dataset of handwritten digits from 0 to 9, each recorded as a 28-by-28 grayscale image. We will use the grayscale values to draw each digit in white over a solid background, preserving the shape and the antialiased edges of its strokes. The backgrounds have different hues but the same target lightness, so the digits have comparable contrast across the palette. We will split the original images before adding any color, using 54,000 digits for training, 6,000 for validation, and the 10,000 images in the official test set for the final evaluation. This keeps different recolorings of the same handwriting from crossing between splits.

Each digit has one preferred color, as shown in the table, and we can decide how much information that color carries by changing how often the two appear together. For every training image, we will assign the preferred color with probability p and divide the remaining probability equally among the other nine colors. For example, when p is 0.95, about 95 out of every 100 training images of a 7 will have the preferred blue background, while the other five will be spread across the rest of the palette. Those five images give the model examples in which the handwriting still says 7 and the color points to another digit.

We will train at seven values of p, from 0.10 through 0.50, 0.70, 0.85, 0.95, and 0.99, up to 1.00. At 0.10, every digit has the same chance of appearing on each of the ten backgrounds, so color and digit are independent by design. At 1.00, every image has its digit’s preferred color, so either shape or color is enough to predict the label in the training data. Across all seven settings we will keep the original training digits, the convolutional network architecture with two convolutional layers, and the ten-epoch training budget fixed, repeating each setting with three pairs of random seeds for the color assignment and model training.

Changing the background at test time

For each of the 10,000 test digits, we will make three versions of the same image. In the aligned version, the background is the preferred color of the true digit. In the independent version, it is drawn uniformly from all ten colors, regardless of the digit, so it can match the preferred color by chance. In the swapped version, we use the preferred color of a particular different digit, pairing 0 with 5, 1 with 6, and so on (n gets paired with n+5 modulo 10). In particular, a 7 gets the preferred color of a 2 and a 2 gets the preferred color of a 7. Every model sees the same three versions of each test image.

We will measure accuracy separately for the three versions and compare the aligned and swapped predictions image by image. The overall flip rate is the fraction of test digits whose prediction changes after the swap. The targeted flip rate asks a more specific question: among the digits that the model classified correctly on their aligned backgrounds, how often does it switch to the digit associated with the new color? For the 7 in the figure, a change from 7 to any other prediction is an overall flip, and a change from 7 specifically to 2 is a targeted one. That second measure helps us see whether the new color is steering the model toward a particular answer.

What should we expect?

At p = 0.10, we expect the model to rely mostly on the handwriting, since each background is equally likely for every digit during training. Its accuracy should therefore be similar across the three versions, and recoloring a correctly classified 7 should rarely turn its prediction into a 2 just because we gave it the color assigned to 2. As p increases, we expect accuracy on the aligned images to stay high, since both cues agree there. Accuracy on independent and swapped backgrounds should fall if the model comes to rely more on color, and we expect targeted flips to become more frequent because the swapped colors consistently point to specific wrong labels.

We might also expect the swapped condition to be harder than the independent one, since an independent color will sometimes agree with the shape, whereas a swapped color always points to another class. How quickly the gap opens is harder to predict. Handwritten digits are recognizable by shape, and even an uncommon mismatched background gives the model an example of the same shape on a different color. We could therefore see the model hold up over much of the sweep and become sensitive to recoloring only when those examples are very rare. Comparing the paired predictions across training settings will let us see how much evidence of this kind the model needs for its predictions to hold up when the background changes.

What the models learned

Across the 21 training runs, the result depended on which version of the test images we used. When each digit appeared on its preferred background, accuracy increased as the association between digit and color grew stronger in training. When we changed the backgrounds, the trend went in the other direction, and the difference between the two scores became especially large in the last two settings. How accuracy changes with the background

When we trained with independent colors at p = 0.10, accuracy was about 98.6% under all three test conditions, and swapping the background changed fewer than three predictions in a thousand. The same digit images gave a very different result as we made color more predictive during training. At p = 0.95, the networks still classified 99.64% of the aligned images correctly, but that number dropped to 86.12% on swapped backgrounds. Raising p to 0.99 brought aligned accuracy slightly higher, to 99.86%, while swapped accuracy fell to 60.87%.

At p = 1.00, every training digit appeared on its preferred background, and all three runs achieved 100% on the aligned images and 0% on the swapped ones. The independent condition gave 13.25% accuracy on average, consistent with an evaluation in which the color is sometimes helpful by chance, while the swapped version always gives the digit a color that was associated with a different label during training. An evaluation using only the aligned images would have ranked the model trained with the strongest color association highest, even though it was the least reliable when the same digits appeared on different backgrounds.

Which digit does the model predict after a swap?

A drop in accuracy tells us that changing the background affects the predictions, but the new predictions also tell us something about how the model uses the cue. At p = 0.99, 33.35% of the digits the models got right on their aligned backgrounds switched specifically to the digit associated with the swapped color. At p = 1.00, every prediction changed, and 95.71% switched to the label that the new color had indicated in training. The remaining changes went to other incorrect classes, so following the color describes the dominant behavior in this setting.

For example, in one of the runs, the model trained at p = 0.99 correctly classified a particular handwritten 9 on its preferred background, then called it a 4 when we gave it the background associated with 4. In the same run, a handwritten 7 remained a 7 under the swap at p = 0.99, but the model trained at p = 1.00 called it a 2 on the background assigned to 2. These examples show how the color cue can determine one prediction while the handwriting still carries enough weight to preserve another, even at the same level of training bias.

What the experiment shows about spurious correlations

There are two changes in the experiment that let us connect the training association to the shortcut. During training, we changed how often each background accompanied its preferred digit while keeping the handwriting examples, network architecture, and training budget fixed. During testing, we changed the background of each image while holding the underlying handwriting and its true label fixed. When the training colors were independent of the labels, the models handled the very same swapped images with high accuracy and rarely predicted the label associated with the new color. As the training association strengthened, recoloring increasingly pushed predictions toward the class that the new color had predicted during training.

Together, those comparisons show that the spurious correlation increased the models’ reliance on color in this experiment. The association was introduced by the way we colored the training images, and it was strong enough to make an incorrect answer predictable when we changed only the background. The biggest losses occurred at the high end of the sweep, where examples that broke the association were scarce or entirely absent. With seven settings, one architecture, and one fixed rule for swapping colors, the experiment establishes the pattern for this setup while leaving open how sharply the change would occur with a finer sweep or a different training procedure.
