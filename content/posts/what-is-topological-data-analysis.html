A handwritten zero can be stretched, tilted, or drawn by a different person, and we still recognize it. The exact coordinates change, but the loop remains. In a dataset, similarly, the arrangement of observations can carry information that is difficult to see by examining one number at a time.

How can we teach a computer to see the shape of data—and distinguish the structures that persist from those that disappear when we change the scale?

Fundamentals of topological data analysis

As R. Buckminster Fuller observed in Operating Manual for Spaceship Earth, “topology is the science of fundamental pattern and structural relationships of event constellations.” [1] What Fuller captured here is something profound. Topology is not concerned with precise distances or specific coordinates, but rather with the qualities of shape and connection that remain invariant as we continuously deform space without tearing or gluing. When we strip away metric information, we expose the skeleton of organization that determines how things are arranged and how they relate to one another, revealing deep structure beneath apparent diversity. Topological data analysis carries forward this same conviction, but applies it where the data itself becomes the source of geometric insight. Gunnar Carlsson extended this vision to the modern problem of understanding high dimensional datasets by articulating that data has shape and the shape matters. [2], [3] When we view a cloud of data points, we are not merely looking at isolated numbers, but at a configuration that encodes intrinsic structure. The shape that emerges from this configuration carries meaning precisely because topology has taught us that shape is the carrier of essential information. Using topological insight, specifically persistent homology, we surface hidden patterns and relationships that can be difficult to recognize from coordinates alone. This is precisely where meaning arises, and from meaning, actionable value follows.

Persistent homology

Let us introduce TDA through an example involving teaching a computer how to differentiate the handwritten numbers \(0\), \(1\), and \(8\). Consider the following representation of such numbers by a discrete set of points in the plane, called a point cloud.

A human being can differentiate such images by identifying that each has a different number of loops, where \(1\) has no loop, \(0\) has one, and \(8\) has two. To make a computer see the number of loops each shape carries using only the cloud of points, we rely on a family of topological objects called simplices, which are assembled into a simplicial complex. We build this complex directly from the point cloud using a construction known as the Vietoris Rips complex. [3] Let \(\{x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n\}\subset \mathbb{R}^d\) be the point cloud in consideration and fix a threshold distance \(\varepsilon\ge0\). The Rips complex is constructed by drawing a ball of radius \(\varepsilon/2\) around each point in the point cloud, and whenever two balls intersect, drawing a line segment connecting their center points.

Mathematically, this yields a simplicial complex where a collection of points forms a \(k\) simplex if and only if every pair among them is within distance \(\varepsilon\). Let us explain what this means. A single point is called a vertex or \(0\) simplex. A line segment connecting two points that are within distance \(\varepsilon\) is called an edge or \(1\) simplex. A filled triangle formed by three points that are pairwise within distance \(\varepsilon\) is called a \(2\) simplex. A filled tetrahedron formed by four such points is called a \(3\) simplex. Generally, a \(k\) simplex is formed by \(k+1\) points such that all \(\binom{k+1}{2}\) pairwise distances are at most \(\varepsilon\). These are abstract simplices; a high-dimensional simplex need not embed in the original ambient space. Pairwise intersections define the Rips rule; requiring a common intersection of all balls instead gives the Čech rule. [3]

The collection of all these simplices is the simplicial complex, called the Vietoris Rips complex whenever generated by this rule. Observe that each value of \(\varepsilon\) produces a complex, which changes only when a pairwise-distance threshold is crossed. When \(\varepsilon\) is small, most points remain isolated as \(0\) simplices with no edges, whereas as \(\varepsilon\) increases, more edges and higher dimensional simplices appear. When \(\varepsilon\) becomes large enough, all points satisfy the pairwise distance criterion and form a single simplex of dimension \(n-1\). The point is that a simplicial complex encodes the geometry of a shape by revealing the presence or absence of holes of different dimensions. The key observation that explains how this works is that loops signal a hole whenever they are not the boundary of a filled region. In fact, if they bound a filled region they do not bound an empty one, which is understood as a hole. To illustrate, consider the following one dimensional example where four points are connected by four edges that form a square.

If no triangles fill the interior, then this square loop detects a genuine hole, meaning an empty region surrounded by this boundary, and we would count a one dimensional hole whose boundary is a loop of \(1\) simplices. But now suppose that we add two triangles to fill the interior.

Then the square loop, while still present in the complex, has become the boundary of a filled region, and it no longer signals a topological hole because the hole has been filled. This procedure repeats itself and allows us to identify holes of different dimensions. At dimension \(k\), the analogue of a closed loop is a formal combination of \(k\) simplices with zero boundary, called a \(k\) cycle. Such a cycle represents a nonzero homology class whenever it does not bound some filled region, represented algebraically by a \((k+1)\) chain. In our example, the outer loop is a \(1\) cycle, and it represents a one dimensional hole if it is not the boundary of the \(2\) chain represented by two triangles in the middle.

We could therefore count the number of holes of dimension \(k\) present in a simplicial complex by counting independent classes of \(k\) cycles modulo boundaries of \((k+1)\) chains. In our starting example, if \(\varepsilon\) is intermediate, we would identify no independent nonbounding \(1\) cycle in the point cloud representing the number \(1\), one in the number \(0\), and two in the number \(8\). This procedure would then be able to tell them apart. However, there is an important caveat worth mentioning. Whenever two cycles bound the same hole, they must be counted only once. Returning to our square example, consider the case where another node is added and we bound the void by two different cycles.

If we were counting holes purely by the number of bounding cycles, we would find that both \(A B E C D A\) and \(A B C D A\) count a hole, leading us to conclude that the figure has two holes when in reality it has only one. This is solved by treating cycles as equivalent, counting them once, whenever they differ only by a filled region. In the example above, notice that the difference between the two cycles is the loop \(B E C B\), which is the boundary of the filled triangle with vertices in \(B\), \(E\), and \(C\). This means that both cycles are effectively lassoing around the same void. The solution to overcounting is then to declare two cycles as equivalent whenever their difference is the boundary of a filled region. So instead of counting holes by the number of cycles, we count equivalence classes of cycles. The number of independent equivalence classes of \(k\) cycles is called the \(k\) th Betti number of the complex, representing the number of independent holes of dimension \(k\). [4]

The algebra behind the holes

What we have just spelled out is the construction of the simplicial homology groups of a given simplicial complex. Mathematically, after choosing coefficients in a field \(\mathbb F\), such as \(\mathbb F_2\), the \(k\) cycles form a vector space \(Z_k\) under formal linear combinations with coefficients in \(\mathbb F\), and \(k\) boundaries constitute a linear subspace \(B_k\). Then the \(k\) th homology group of the simplicial chain complex \(C_\bullet(K;\mathbb F)\) is the quotient space

\[H_k(K;\mathbb F)=Z_k/B_k\]

whose elements are equivalence classes of \(k\) cycles. The number of \(k\) dimensional holes is then the dimension of this space

\[\beta_k=\dim_{\mathbb F}H_k(K;\mathbb F)\]

which we call the \(k\) th Betti number of \(K\) with coefficients in \(\mathbb F\). With integer coefficients, these are abelian groups rather than vector spaces, and Betti numbers are their ranks. Homology groups are topological invariants: they are preserved by homeomorphisms, and more generally by homotopy equivalences, rather than by arbitrary continuous transformations. [4]

The key insight that originates persistent homology is that as \(\varepsilon\) varies, the complex evolves, holes emerge and disappear, and those that persist across a substantial range of values for \(\varepsilon\) are candidates for robust topological features of the underlying space. [3], [5] In our starting example with the numbers \(0\), \(1\), and \(8\), as we vary \(\varepsilon\), we expect that \(\beta_0\) will quickly become \(1\) in every example once all nodes are connected, and that the behavior of \(\beta_1\) will determine the classification. We expect \(\beta_1=0\) to be persistent for \(1\) since there are no relevant loops, \(\beta_1=1\) to be persistent for \(0\) due to its single loop, and \(\beta_1=2\) to be persistent for \(8\) because of its two loops.

Summarizing, given a point cloud, persistent homology consists of computing the simplicial homology of the Vietoris Rips complex for each distance threshold \(\varepsilon\ge0\), together with the maps induced by the inclusions between these complexes. Geometric insight is generated by keeping track of how homology classes emerge and vanish as the threshold grows and the complex is updated. It is the long-lived classes, rather than Betti numbers themselves, that record persistent features in the shape of data. We emphasize the word persistent because small deviations in the configuration of the point cloud can create transient geometric structures that do not represent true features. A natural representation for this multiscale information is the persistence diagram.

Sublevel sets and filtrations

While the Vietoris Rips complex naturally extracts topology from discrete point clouds by increasing a radius parameter, a more general mechanism to generate such nested sequences is the sublevel set filtration. Suppose we are given a fixed, global simplicial complex \(K\) and a real valued function \(f\) mapping \(K\) to \(\mathbb{R}\) defined on its simplices. To ensure topological consistency, we require \(f\) to be monotonic, meaning that if a simplex \(\sigma\) is a face of \(\tau\), then \(f(\sigma) \le f(\tau)\). For any real threshold \(t\), we define the sublevel set complex \(K_t\) as the collection of all simplices whose function value is at most \(t\).

\[K_t = \{\sigma \in K \mid f(\sigma) \le t\}\]

Because of the monotonicity of \(f\), if a simplex belongs to \(K_t\), all of its faces also belong to \(K_t\), ensuring that \(K_t\) is a valid simplicial subcomplex. Furthermore, as we increase the threshold \(t\), these subcomplexes are naturally nested. If \(s \le t\), then \(K_s \subseteq K_t \subseteq K\). This nested sequence of spaces forms a filtration. For a finite complex, it changes only at finitely many threshold values. [3]

The inclusion maps between these nested complexes naturally induce linear transformations between their corresponding homology groups at each dimension \(k\).

\[i_{s,t}^k: H_k(K_s) \to H_k(K_t)\]

Instead of merely looking at the homology of a single complex at an isolated scale, persistent homology studies the persistent homology groups, which are defined mathematically as the images of these induced maps.

\[H_k^{s,t} = \operatorname{im}(i_{s,t}^k)\]

The dimension of this vector space is the persistent Betti number, denoted by

\[\beta_k^{s,t} = \operatorname{dim}(H_k^{s,t})\]

The meaning of this number is fundamentally tied to the multiscale nature of TDA. For a finite filtration with field coefficients, it counts the independent persistence intervals of dimension \(k\), representing connected components, loops, or voids, that were born at or before \(s\) and are still alive at \(t\). With the usual half-open interval convention \([b,d)\), this means \(b\le s\le t\lt d\). In other words, it quantifies the topological structures that persist across the parameter interval from \(s\) to \(t\), helping distinguish long-lived features from transient ones. Persistence alone does not determine whether a feature is meaningful signal or noise. [3], [5] This algebraic formulation rigorously tracks how homological classes emerge and vanish as the filtration progresses, directly yielding the birth and death coordinates that populate the persistence diagram.

Birth, death, and persistence diagrams

As mentioned earlier, as the filtration parameter increases, the complex changes. At small values, the components are mostly disjoint. As the parameter grows, these components begin to merge, while new loops may appear and later disappear as more simplices are added. Thus, rather than considering the topology of the data at only one particular scale, we can study how its topological features evolve throughout the entire filtration. For a fixed dimension \(k\), each interval in the persistence barcode can therefore be described by two values. The first is its birth time, the threshold at which the class first appears, and the second is its death time, the threshold at which the class disappears. The difference between these two values measures how long the corresponding topological feature persists throughout the filtration.

\[\text{persistence} = \text{death} - \text{birth}\]

A loop that exists only for a very small range of threshold values is considered less persistent than one that survives over a larger range. This information can be represented graphically by a persistence diagram. For each finite interval, we plot a point \((b,d)\) in the plane, where \(b\) is its birth time and \(d\) is its death time. The horizontal coordinate records when the feature appears, while the vertical coordinate records when it disappears. The further a point lies from the diagonal \(d=b\), the longer the corresponding topological feature persists, while points closer to the diagonal have short persistence and are often associated with small scale fluctuations or noise in the data. A diagram for \(H_0\) records the appearance and merging of connected components, while a diagram for \(H_1\) records the appearance and disappearance of loops. Higher dimensional persistence diagrams similarly record the evolution of higher dimensional homological features. For instance, if the following is a diagram for \(H_1\)

Summarization via persistence images

A central goal of TDA is to extract qualitative geometric features, such as connected components, loops, and voids, from complex and noisy datasets. As seen in the previous section, the tool that encodes this information is the persistence diagram, which displays such features by tracking the scale at which those topological structures appear and disappear. When extracting topological information from datasets to train a machine learning model, a straightforward approach is to calculate fundamental scalar statistics. For instance, one might compute scalar summaries such as feature counts, total or maximum persistences, and persistence entropy. While these aggregate metrics are simple to implement and often work well in practice, something crucial is lost in translation. Condensing the data strips away the nuanced multiscale distribution of geometric structures that a full persistence diagram records. To preserve the richness provided by TDA, we would ideally use the entire diagrams as training data for the models. The problem is that persistence diagrams are unstructured multisets of points in the plane that lack a natural inner product structure, and their metrics cannot be used directly by many standard algorithms that expect fixed-length feature vectors. [6] This is where summarization techniques come into play.

For persistence images, summarizing a persistence diagram means mapping it into a well behaved vector space, namely a finite dimensional Hilbert space, where standard operations like computing distances and inner products become tractable. This allows one to retain more of the multiscale distribution than a few scalar summaries, although a fixed-resolution representation can still lose information. Among these, persistence images stand out as a particularly elegant and practical solution. The core idea is to transform the scattered discrete points of a diagram into a stable fixed resolution matrix, retaining information about the distribution of features in the original diagram while delivering the structured vectorizable format required by machine learning pipelines. The word stable is emphasized here because we cannot rely on just any arbitrary method of flattening a diagram into a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^n\). To be practically useful, we need to ensure that if two persistence diagrams are structurally alike, their corresponding matrices also have to be close to one another as vectors. In other words, the transformation from persistence diagrams to \(\mathbb{R}^N\) has to be continuous with respect to the metric being used in \(\mathbb{R}^N\) and a suitable metric on the set of persistence diagrams. The standard persistence-image stability estimate uses the 1-Wasserstein distance; a bottleneck estimate additionally requires control of the number of diagram points. [6]

A natural first attempt to vectorize a persistence diagram would be to overlay an \(m \times n\) grid onto the plane and assign each pixel a value equal to the sum of the persistences of the points falling inside it. This provides a straightforward way to view a persistence diagram as an \((m\cdot n)\) dimensional vector or an \(m\times n\) matrix. The problem with this approach is that the resulting representation is not continuous, meaning it is not stable under small perturbations in the data. Indeed, because the grid boundaries are rigid, a very small perturbation could shift a point across a pixel boundary, causing an abrupt jump in the resulting matrix and violating the core principle that similar diagrams must produce similar vector representations.

The solution is to replace the assignment of discrete points to rigid bins with a continuous spatial distribution. Specifically, we place a continuous kernel, typically a two dimensional Gaussian, centered at each point in the diagram. We then construct a smooth global density surface by aggregating these kernels via a weighted sum, where the weight typically increases with the persistence of the point and vanishes when persistence is zero. Think of this process as building a landscape over the diagram, called a persistence surface. [6] Instead of flat ground with isolated rigid spikes, we mold a continuous topography of hills and valleys, where the height of each peak reflects both the location and the importance of the corresponding topological feature.

The value assigned to each pixel of the grid is then the volume of the persistence surface lying directly over it. Intuitively, this continuous approach solves the instability problem, as small shifts in the diagram merely cause small proportional variations in the volume above each pixel, completely avoiding the abrupt jumps inherent to the corresponding naive discrete matrices.

Precisely, let \(D \subset \mathbb{R}^2\) be a persistence diagram, viewed as a multiset of points \((b, d)\) representing the birth and death times of topological features. For computational and stability reasons, it is standard to apply the linear transformation \(T(b,d)=(b,d-b)\), where \(p=d-b\) denotes the persistence of the feature. Write \(\widetilde D=T(D)\) for the transformed diagram, with coordinates \((b,p)\). We use only finite birth and death coordinates here; essential classes, with infinite death, must be handled separately. The persistence surface associated with \(D\) is the height function \(\rho_D: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}\) given by

\[\rho_D(x,y)=\sum_{(b_i,p_i)\in \widetilde D} w(b_i,p_i)K_\sigma(x-b_i,y-p_i)\]

where \(w: \mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty) \to [0,\infty)\) is a weighting function, designed to vanish along the horizontal axis. A simple choice is \(w(b,p)=p\) over a bounded persistence range; a bounded Lipschitz extension such as \(w(b,p)=\min(p,P)\), for a fixed \(P\gt 0\), meets the assumptions used below. The function \(K_\sigma\) is a two dimensional Gaussian kernel

\[K_\sigma(x,y)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}\exp\left(-\frac{x^2+y^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\]

Next, choose a fixed rectangular domain \(R=[x_{\min},x_{\max}] \times [y_{\min},y_{\max}]\) covering the transformed diagrams of interest, and partition it into an \(m \times n\) grid of pixels, denoted \(\{C_{i,j}\}\) for \(1 \le i \le m\) and \(1 \le j \le n\). For each cell, we compute the volume of the persistence surface lying over it

\[V_{i,j}=\iint_{C_{i,j}}\rho_D(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\]

The persistence image of \(D\) is then defined as the \(m \times n\) matrix

\[\operatorname{PI}(D)=(V_{i,j})_{i,j}\]

which can equivalently be flattened into a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^{m \cdot n}\). The kernel width, weight, domain, and grid must be held fixed when comparing diagrams; Gaussian tails outside the domain are omitted. [6]

To formally state the stability of this transformation, we first define two standard metrics used to compare persistence diagrams. Let \(D_1\) and \(D_2\) be two persistence diagrams, each augmented with the diagonal \(\Delta=\{(x,x) \mid x \in \mathbb{R}\}\) taken with infinite multiplicity. The bottleneck distance between them is defined as

\[d_B(D_1,D_2)=\inf_{\gamma} \sup_{u \in D_1} \|u-\gamma(u)\|_\infty\]

where the infimum is taken over all possible bijections \(\gamma: D_1 \to D_2\).

Why persistence images are stable

The bottleneck distance measures the largest displacement in a matching. The 1-Wasserstein distance instead measures the total displacement:

\[W_1(D_1,D_2)=\inf_{\gamma}\sum_{u\in D_1}\|u-\gamma(u)\|_\infty.\]

As above, both diagrams are augmented with the diagonal, so a feature may be matched to the diagonal when it disappears. Terms matching diagonal points to themselves contribute zero. [5], [6]

Stability of Persistence Images

Fix a Gaussian kernel with \(\sigma\gt 0\) and a finite grid of pixels of bounded area. Suppose that the weight function \(w\) is bounded and Lipschitz continuous and vanishes along the horizontal axis, \(w(b,0)=0\). Then there is a constant \(C\gt 0\), depending on the weight, kernel, and grid, such that for any two finite persistence diagrams with finite coordinates,

\[\|\operatorname{PI}(D_1)-\operatorname{PI}(D_2)\|_\infty\le C\,W_1(D_1,D_2).\]

This is a sufficient-assumptions version of persistence-image stability; the standard result is proved by Adams and collaborators. [6]

Proof

Let \(\gamma:D_1\to D_2\) be a matching. By the triangle inequality, the absolute difference in the surfaces is bounded by the sum of the differences of the individual weighted kernels evaluated at matched pairs \(u\) and \(v=\gamma(u)\).

Write \(W=\|w\|_\infty\) and \(M=\|K_\sigma\|_\infty\), and let \(L_w\) and \(L_K\) be their Lipschitz constants with respect to the infinity norm. The Gaussian kernel is bounded and globally Lipschitz. If \(z=(x,y)\), the difference between the contributions of two transformed points \(Tu\) and \(Tv\) satisfies

\[\begin{aligned} &|w(Tu)K_\sigma(z-Tu)-w(Tv)K_\sigma(z-Tv)|\\ &\qquad\le (L_wM+WL_K)\|Tu-Tv\|_\infty\\ &\qquad\le 2(L_wM+WL_K)\|u-v\|_\infty. \end{aligned}\]

The factor \(2\) comes from the birth-persistence transformation \(T(b,d)=(b,d-b)\). If a point is matched to the diagonal, its partner has zero weight, so the same estimate still applies.

Summing over the matched pairs and taking the infimum over matchings gives

\[\|\rho_{D_1}-\rho_{D_2}\|_\infty\le 2(L_wM+WL_K)W_1(D_1,D_2).\]

Finally, if \(A\) is the maximum area of a pixel, integrating this bound over each cell yields

\[\|\operatorname{PI}(D_1)-\operatorname{PI}(D_2)\|_\infty\le 2A(L_wM+WL_K)W_1(D_1,D_2),\]

which concludes the proof.

If each diagram has at most \(N\) off-diagonal points, a bottleneck matching has at most \(2N\) pairs involving an off-diagonal point. Consequently,

\[W_1(D_1,D_2)\le 2N\,d_B(D_1,D_2),\]

and the same argument gives a bottleneck bound whose constant depends on \(N\). This qualification matters: the differences of individual kernels are summed, so the number of points cannot simply be discarded from a bottleneck estimate.
