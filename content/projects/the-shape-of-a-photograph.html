An image has more than one kind of shape

We usually describe a photograph by what it depicts: a face, a landscape, an object. But an image also has a mathematical structure. Regard its grayscale intensities as a landscape, and dark regions become valleys. As a threshold rises, regions appear and merge; loops form and disappear. Persistent homology records this changing structure.

This project asks whether those topological patterns carry useful information about an image’s origin. Using CIFAKE, a dataset of real and synthetic images, I compare two ways of turning persistent homology into inputs for machine learning: a small collection of numerical summaries and a pair of two-dimensional surfaces. [1]

For the mathematical background, read What is topological data analysis? A comprehensive introduction.

From pixels to topology

  1. Build an intensity landscape. Convert each 32 × 32 image to grayscale and normalize intensities to \([0,1]\).
  2. Follow a cubical filtration. Use GUDHI to compute persistent connected components, \(H_0\), and loops, \(H_1\), as the intensity threshold increases. Retain finite persistence intervals. [3]
  3. Summarize the diagrams. Extract 16 descriptors, including counts, lifetime statistics, and persistence entropy, for six tabular classifiers.
  4. Keep more of the diagram’s structure. Weight points by their lifetimes, smooth them with Gaussians, and sample one 32 × 32 surface per homology degree. Feed the two channels into TopoCNN, a residual convolutional network with 122,914 trainable parameters.
  5. Compare and interpret. Evaluate the saved models on a shared internal holdout and use SHAP to explore the scalar model’s feature attributions.

The distinction matters: the standard persistence-image construction integrates a surface over grid cells. This experiment samples it at grid points, with Gaussian bandwidth \(\sigma=0.05\). The introductory article explains the standard construction; the report documents the implementation used here. [2]

What the experiment found

The input pool comprises 20,000 images—10,000 real and 10,000 synthetic—from the upstream CIFAKE test partition. The scripts create an internal 80/20 split: 16,000 training-pool images and 4,000 holdout images. TopoCNN uses 14,400 of the training-pool images for fitting and 1,600 for validation. These results therefore describe this internal experiment, rather than CIFAKE’s official train/test benchmark.

RBF SVM achieved the highest scalar-model accuracy; LightGBM achieved the highest scalar-model ROC-AUC. TopoCNN improved both measures in this saved run. “Real” is the positive class, for which its recall was 81.75%. The complete seven-model comparison is available in the results CSV.

What these results mean

The experiment provides evidence that topological summaries carry information useful for this classification task. The stronger result with sampled surfaces suggests a promising direction for further work, although both the representation and the classifier change, so their effects cannot be isolated here.

It remains an exploratory study: there is no matched raw-pixel CNN baseline, evaluation across generators, or robustness study under compression and blur. Some exploratory analysis also includes the holdout, and the original CNN does not seed initialization or minibatch shuffling. A fresh evaluation on untouched data is needed before drawing broader conclusions. Feature attributions describe the model’s predictions; they do not establish physical causes of the differences between real and synthetic images.

The public report preserves the saved results and figures while clarifying the evaluation protocol, the sampled-surface implementation, and the limits of interpretation. Its publication notes record these changes. No new model training was performed for this edition.

Code & materials

The downloadable workspace contains the Python pipeline, the editable LaTeX report and compiled PDF, every report figure, saved metric tables, held-out predictions, and a small offline verification script. It also includes a README, dependency files, citation metadata, and a portable editor workspace.
